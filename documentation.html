01

Overview

The Stolen Archive (the Looted Items Collection) brings together thirty of the world's best-known artefacts that are linked by a history of colonialism, war and looting. It shows how they were made, what they meant to the cultures that made them, how they were taken, and where the question of their return stands today.

The site works as a companion to a visit to a physical museum and as a complete virtual museum. The visitor decides how to move through the collection: which of three tours to follow, how long and how complex the texts should be, and in which language to read them.
Objects30, numbered 001–030
Tours3: Timeline, Colonial Conquests, Geo
Texts per object and tour9: three lengths × three complexity levels
Texts in total810 (30 objects × 3 tours × 9 versions)
Museum2 floors, 13 rooms (12 in use)
ThemesDark and light

The project was developed for the course Information Modelling and Web Technologies (prof. Fabio Vitali), Master's Degree in Digital Humanities and Digital Knowledge, University of Bologna.

03

Visual identity

The design is called The Inventory: the order of a Swiss museum catalogue with a maximalist layer on top. Swiss design gives the structure (grid, numbering, labels, flush-left text, hairline rules); the maximalist layer gives the drama (full-width display type, outlined numerals, the engraved Parthenon drawings, a moving band of object names and tinted photographs).

Grid and spacing

A 12-column grid, 1440px wide at most, with gutters of 16–24px and outer margins of 16–56px (.grid in site.css). On screens narrower than 800px it becomes 4 columns. Sections are separated by 1px rules and generous vertical space instead of boxes.

Typography

Karrik exists only in a Regular cut, which reads as bold at text sizes, so paragraphs use Space Grotesk, a grotesque with a similar character. The type scale runs from 13px labels through an 18px body and a 22px lead to fluid display sizes (--step-1 … --step-hero), which grow with the window.

Images and motifs

  • Inventory numbers (001–030) appear on every page as outlined Karrik numerals and in mono labels.
  • Engravings: the three Parthenon drawings (partenone1–3.svg) are used as CSS masks, so they take the theme colour.
  • Duotone photographs: in grids and strips, photos are shown in greyscale tinted with the theme colour, and turn to full colour on hover or keyboard focus.
  • Stamps: rotated vermilion labels such as “Removed from Athens” or “Returned 2021”.

Motion

The title rises in on load, the object strip and sections fade in as they scroll into view, a band of object names scrolls across the home page (with a Pause button), and the removal arcs on the maps flow slowly. All motion stops when the visitor's system asks for reduced motion.

04

Accessibility
  • One <h1> per page and a heading outline that follows the content.
  • A “Skip to content” link as the first focusable element.
  • A visible 3px vermilion focus ring on every interactive element.
  • Real buttons for every control: the menu button reports aria-expanded; the theme, tour, length and complexity buttons report aria-pressed; era tabs use role="tab" and move with the arrow keys.
  • The moving list of objects can be paused, and every animation stops under prefers-reduced-motion.
  • The maps have text equivalents: the Atlas lists every route and every room next to the maps, and each object page gives origin, current location and room as text in the museum label.
  • The map pages do not capture the mouse wheel, so scrolling the page never gets stuck inside a map.
  • Colour contrast of at least 4.5:1 for text in both themes; Windows high-contrast mode is supported.
  • The page language changes with the chosen translation (<html lang>).
05

Tours and personalization

Three tours are the core ways into the collection. Each visits all thirty objects, in its own order and with its own texts.

TourFocusOrder
TimelineThe historical and artistic value of the objects.Chronological, from the oldest object to the most recent.
Colonial Conquests and Cultural LossThe social and political consequences of colonialism and looting.Alphabetical, each object read through the conquest or war in which it was taken.
GeoWhere the objects come from and the events tied to their creation and removal.Continent by continent: Africa, the Americas, Asia, Europe, Oceania.

Complexity

  • Fun: simple, engaging language that avoids technical terms without losing information. Meant for young visitors and school groups.
  • Basic: more context and specific information, still light in tone and understandable without previous knowledge.
  • Expert: historical detail, references to specific events, debates and scholarship, for students, academics and informed amateurs.

Length

  • Short: the general context, concise.
  • Medium: more detail and context.
  • Long: the full narrative with dates, events and implications.

Length and complexity combine freely, so every object has nine versions of its text in each tour. The visitor chooses them on the Tours page before starting and can change them at any time on the object page.

07

The collection

The objects were chosen from all over the world and from different periods, to show that colonization and looting took many forms across time and space. All of them were removed from their original sites, by armies, collectors, archaeologists, diplomats or the market; some were given as gifts and later dispersed. Several have since been returned, see Perspectives.

The collection started with fifteen objects. In September 2026 the Gdańsk astronomical clock was removed (it was evacuated for safety in 1944 but never looted), the Beijing Observatory instruments took its number, 006, and fifteen objects were added as 016–030, on a second museum floor.

09

Object page

The object page shows one object at a time within a tour. From top to bottom:

  1. Tour bar: the tour's name and position (“01 / 30”), a switch between the three tours, and previous / next buttons.
  2. Title: the inventory number as a large outlined numeral, the object's name, and its date on a separate line.
  3. 3D view and gallery: when a real 3D scan exists, the object opens with it (see 3D view); the first thumbnail, “3D”, returns to it. The other thumbnails show the object's photos (1–4 each), with author and licence under the photo; the arrows beside the counter (or the arrow keys) step through all of them. Enlarge, or a click on the photo, opens a full-screen viewer (images-vis.js) with its own previous and next, wheel zoom and drag; Escape closes it on the photo last shown.
  4. Museum label: a column that stays in view, with the inventory number, where the object was taken from, where it is held, its room, the length and complexity controls and, when translations exist, a language menu.
  5. Text: the tour's text for the chosen length and complexity.
  6. Perspectives: see Perspectives.
  7. Go deeper: history and geography panels, shown only when llm-data.json exists (see LLM enrichment).
  8. Then & now: the floor plan with the object's pin (on the object's floor; the floor switch shows the other floor), and the world map zoomed to its journey.
  9. The record: the RDF metadata as a diagram, and the Dublin Core table (see Metadata).
  10. Strip: 01–30, one button per object in the tour.

How it works

object-page.js reads the visitor's choices from localStorage (key tourData: tour name, length, complexity), fetches tour-data.json and shows the item at currentIndex. displayItem() fills in every part of the page; the length and complexity buttons save the new choice and call it again, and the tour buttons save the new tour and reload from its first object.

The page also accepts parameters in its address, read by getQueryParams(), so any object can be linked directly:

ParameterExampleEffect
no?no=5Opens inventory number 005 in the current tour.
tour?tour=Geo%20TourSwitches to that tour (keeps length and complexity unless they are given too).

10

Perspectives

Each object page has a section asking whose object it is. It draws on two kinds of source.

Two sides

For each object, perspectives.json records the position of the community or state of origin, the position of the institution that holds it, and the current status, each with the published sources it is drawn from (official statements, news reports, parliamentary records, museum pages). The status is one of: contested, in talks, returned, partly returned, dispersed, no formal claim found, or no removal found. Returned objects also change the stamp in the museum label (for example “Returned 2021”).

These texts are researched and written by hand, not generated. Positions attributed to each side are theirs; the site does not declare a winner. The file records the date of the research (September 2026); positions change, so the sources should be checked again before publishing.

Three tours, three readings

Below the two sides, the page shows how each of the three tours tells the same object, at the visitor's current complexity level, with a link to read it inside the other tours. The tours frame each object differently (as art and history, as conquest, as geography), and seeing them side by side is part of the argument.

What the research changed

  • The Throne of King Glele was returned to Benin by France in 2021.
  • Yale returned the Machu Picchu collection to Peru between 2011 and 2012.
  • The Achaemenid relief from the Montreal Museum of Fine Arts was returned to Iran after its seizure in New York in 2017.
  • The Gdańsk astronomical clock was never taken abroad: it was dismantled and evacuated for safety in 1944 and reconstructed in St Mary's Church. It was removed from the collection and replaced by the Beijing Observatory instruments.
  • Among the new objects, the Paracas textiles (2014–2021), the Uigwe (2011, on renewable loan) and Kalaniʻōpuʻu's cloak and helmet (2016, permanently 2020) have been returned; Ngonnso's return was agreed in 2022 but is still pending.
  • Five pins on the floor plan did not match their room captions and were moved into the right rooms, and Tipu's Tiger's caption was corrected to Room 2, as in the room list above.
11

Fairness review

In September 2026 the site was reviewed from the point of view of the communities the objects come from, looking for places where the way it names, groups or describes things tilts the story. These are the problems found and what was changed.

Changed

  • Places named at different scales. Some objects were said to come from a city (Athens), others from a whole country (“India”, “Egypt”, “Nigeria”) or an old empire (“Persia”). Every object now follows one pattern: the place, the historical state in brackets when it helps, and the present-day country, for example “Srirangapatna (Kingdom of Mysore), India”. The present-day country only locates the place; it is not a statement about who the heritage belongs to.
  • Errors and ambiguities in origins. Tipu's Tiger was captioned as coming from Persepolis; it comes from Srirangapatna. “Benin (Nigeria)” could be read as the Republic of Benin; the Benin Bronzes and the mask of Queen Idia now say Benin City (Kingdom of Benin), Nigeria, and the throne of Glele says Abomey (Kingdom of Dahomey), Benin. The Koh-i-Noor was surrendered at Lahore, in today's Pakistan, not in “India”; its earlier history, through several South and Central Asian courts, is told in the texts.
  • One room named after a continent. Room 5 was “African Heritage” (captioned “African Artifacts”), while Greek and Egyptian works were “Ancient Civilizations” and no other continent had a room of its own. The Nok terracottas, as old as the Parthenon, moved to Room 1, Ancient Civilizations; Room 5 is now Royal Workshops, for the Benin casters' guild. “Treasures of the Americas” became Feather, Gold and Cloth, “Pacific Encounters” became Gifts and Seizures, and “Lost Cities and Civilizations” became Cities and Palaces.
  • Continents. The Altar of Pergamon was listed under Europe, although Pergamon is in Anatolia (Asia), where Troy is listed. Every object is now placed by where it was taken from.
  • How each object left. “Looted” does not fit every case. Each object now states how it left in one line (seized in war, surrendered under a treaty, removed under a permit, excavated and divided, sold, smuggled, given), shown on the object page, in the collection and in the table above. The home page asks where the objects were “taken”, not “looted”, from.
  • The “discovery” of Machu Picchu. The texts called Hiram Bingham the discoverer of a “lost city”. Quechua families were farming the site in 1911 and a local farmer guided him there. The 27 Machu Picchu texts were rewritten; they also no longer say that the objects went to Europe, or that Peru is still asking for objects that came back in 2011–2012.
  • Names. Rapa Nui comes before “Easter Island”, the moai is called by its Rapa Nui name, Hoa Hakananai'a, and the Parthenon sculptures are not called after Lord Elgin. Each object has the same name in all three tours.
  • Returned objects on the map. A city that only held objects that have since gone back is shown as a hollow square, “former holder”, instead of looking like the place where they are today.
  • Colonial and other takings. The Colonial Conquests tour now says that it also includes objects taken in wars between European states, so that colonial looting can be compared with other cases instead of being confused with them.

Still to do

  • Most sources are in English and many come from holding institutions or Western media; the perspectives would be fairer with more sources in the languages of the communities of origin.
  • Translations are offered in Italian, French and Spanish, which are European languages. Arabic, Chinese, Korean, Hindi, Persian, Greek, Turkish and Indigenous languages such as Quechua or ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi would serve the communities of origin better.
  • Many of the original 2024 texts still use the museum language of the time (“artifacts”, “treasures”, “explorers”). The texts written in 2026 use it less; the older ones should be revised in the same way.
  • The museum and the three tours present the holders' photographs; images made or chosen by communities of origin would change who is looking at whom.

Maps as inline SVG

Both maps are drawn directly in the HTML, not loaded as images. One drawing serves every object; only the markers move. This replaced 31 separate SVG files (about 22 MB).

Museum floor plan

The walls, the room numbers and a pin are paths inside <svg class="museum-map">. Each floor is a group, <g class="museum-floor" data-floor="1"> and data-floor="2", drawn on the same outline so that the pins use one coordinate system. Each object has a floor and a pin position in tour-data.json:

"maps": { "museum-pin": { "x": 110, "y": 506 }, "museum-floor": 1, "caption-1": "Room 1 - Ancient Civilizations" }

The floor switch above the plan (.floor-switch, tabs with arrow-key support) is wired by setupFloors() in inventory.js, which shows one floor group and hides the other. object-page.js opens the plan on the object's floor and moves the pin with transform="translate(x y)"; the pin is hidden while the other floor is shown. The colours come from CSS (.museum-map-walls, .museum-map-pin), so the plan follows the theme.

World map

The land is a single path generated from the country outlines of world.geo.json (Natural Earth): an equirectangular projection onto a 1000 × 394 box (longitude −180…180, latitude 84…−58), Antarctica left out, outlines simplified with the Ramer–Douglas–Peucker algorithm to about 64 KB. The same projection is in inventory.js:

x = (lng + 180) / 360 * 1000
y = (84 - lat) / 142 * 394

renderGeoMap() places a circle at the place of origin and a square at the holding city, draws a curved arc between them, and changes the SVG's viewBox to frame the journey, keeping markers and labels the same size on screen. Coordinates come from inventory.js: origins from the site's original map, destinations as city centres.

3D view

When a real 3D scan of an object is openly available, its page opens with it; the first thumbnail, “3D”, returns to it from the photos. Nine objects have one. The others show their photos only: making shapes up from a photograph (an earlier version of the site tried depth estimation) gave inflated, misleading models, and there were not enough photos from all sides to reconstruct them.

The scans

The scans come from Scan the World (MyMiniFactory) and other open collections, downloaded from Zenodo, which keeps a copy of each with its licence. models/manifest.json lists, for each, what the scan shows, whether it is the object itself or a related piece, the author, the licence and the source. Most are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (non-commercial, which this course project is); Nefertiti's is CC0. Two are not the object itself and say so under the model: a relief still in place at Persepolis, and a 19th-century marble copy of the Dendera Zodiac.

The viewer

viewer3d.js uses three.js (loaded from jsDelivr as an ES module, with the Draco decoder for the compressed scans). Every scan is shown in one matte material in the theme's ink colour, lit from the upper left with a thin vermilion rim light, and follows the dark and light themes; when a scan has its own texture, Colour shows it. Drag to rotate, scroll or pinch to zoom; with the keyboard, the arrow keys rotate and + and − zoom. Reset view returns to the start. The model turns slowly until the visitor touches it, and not at all when the system asks for reduced motion. Without WebGL the page says so and shows the photos.

14

Atlas

The Atlas replaces the former Map page and the map on the home page with one view of the whole collection.

  • World: a Leaflet map (atlas-map.js, also used on the home page) with a point for each place of origin, a square for each holding city and an arc for each removal; arcs of returned objects are dotted. Next to it, the list of objects: hovering or focusing an object highlights its route and dims the others; selecting it opens its page.
  • Museum: the two floor plans with a numbered marker for every object and, next to them, the rooms with their objects, grouped by floor. Hovering an object in any list highlights it on the maps, switching to its floor when needed.

The arc is a quadratic Bézier curve between origin and destination, bent to one side by 22% of the distance, drawn as a Leaflet polyline of 48 points.

Metadata

Every object is described twice: with Dublin Core for a general description, and as an RDF graph based on CIDOC CRM for its history and movements.

Dublin Core table

Each object has an HTML table in Items_JS_HTML/ with dc:title, dc:creator, dc:subject, dc:type, dc:description, dcterms:created and dcterms:spatial: authors, date and place of creation, subjects and title. The table's path is stored in tour-data.json (metadata.Table); object-page.js fetches it and inserts it into the page.

RDF graph

Each object has an RDF/XML file in rdf_files_graphs/ built on CIDOC CRM (for example E22 human-made object or E24 physical human-made thing, E53 place, P55 has current location), combined with Dublin Core, Wikidata, GeoNames and WGS84 coordinates, plus a small project vocabulary (items-ontology) where no existing term fitted. On the object page the RDF is drawn as a 2D graph (graph2d.js, from graphs/NN.json, made by tools/rdf_graph_json.py).

  • The graph reads from left to right: the object, then its statements in five groups (made, origin, removal, today, described as), then one node per value, with the CIDOC CRM and Dublin Core properties in plain words (P52 is owned by, P45 made of). It is drawn with d3 (tree layout) in SVG.
  • A resource (a person, place, event or institution) that has statements of its own shows +N; selecting it opens a further branch, and Open all opens every branch. A long value, such as the description, shows read and opens a note with the full text. Hovering a node lights the path back to the object.
  • All statements are also listed as text under the graph.

LLM enrichment

An optional script, llm/enrich.mjs, uses Claude (Anthropic API) to draft extra content from the site's own texts. It runs on a computer, not in the browser, so no API key is ever published; it writes llm-data.json, which the object page loads if it exists.
